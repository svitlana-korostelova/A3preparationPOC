18.1. Banking interview — project story and soft skills

They let Q1 run for about 3 minutes without interrupting. For S1–S5 they ask once more: "and who decides?" or "what if that doesn't work?"

Q1. Tell me about your last project: one technical problem you solved, and what you decided.

Shape, not a script. They are scoring English and whether the story is yours. If it sounds memorised they will change a detail ("now the screen is opened from a deep link — what changes?") and ask why.

  1. One sentence on the product and your role.
  2. One concrete problem: a bug, a constraint, or a design choice. Name the file or the screen, not "we improved performance".
  3. The decision you made. Say "I", not "we", for that step.
  4. The trade-off: what you gave up, and why that was acceptable.
  5. The result, in one sentence. Stop. Do not fill the rest of the 3 minutes with a second story.

Use a problem you can still draw on a whiteboard. A deep-link follow-up should be answerable from the same design: who owns navigation, what state is missing, what you would test.

S1. Three days before the sprint ends, the requirements change a lot. The deadline stays. What do you do?

Say this: I raise it the same day, in the ticket, with options. I do not decide the deadline myself, and I do not quietly work nights to hide it.

  • Write the risk where the PO and the BA can see it: new UI, new API contract, three days left.
  • Offer options, each with what ships and what does not: cut the scope, split the ticket, ship a basic version and put the rest in the next sprint.
  • Who decides: the PO, with the client. I estimate and recommend. I do not promise the full new scope.

If that does not work: I ask which of the options they want in writing, then I do that option. I do not start the full redesign while the decision is open.

S2. A ticket has an empty description and a link to the whole Figma file. What do you do?

Say this: Clarifying it is my job. I do not wait for someone to "describe it", and I do not build the whole file.

  • Read the Figma far enough to write a short proposal: which screens, which states, what I think is out of scope.
  • Ask the reporter first, then the PO or BA, with those specific questions.
  • Put the agreed slice back on the ticket before I estimate or code.

If nobody answers: I say I am blocked, name who I asked, and pick the smallest slice I am willing to defend. I do not invent the API contract.

S3. Halfway through the sprint you realise the ticket needs one more sprint. What do you do?

Say this: I say it as soon as I know, with the reason and a new estimate. I also say what can still land in this sprint.

  • The reason is specific: a wrong assumption, a hidden dependency, a review that took longer. Not "it was harder than I thought" on its own.
  • A split: this sprint gets the part that is done and safe; the rest gets a new estimate.
  • Who decides to cut or to slip: the PO. I do not move the sprint end by myself, and I do not stay silent until the review.

S4. A colleague rejects your PR for another approach and will not discuss it. What do you do?

Say this: I start from the written guideline, then I ask for a short call. If they still refuse, I ask the lead or a third iOS engineer to choose. After that I update the guideline so we do not repeat the thread.

  • Re-read the team rules first (architecture, "small change to legacy code", test expectations). The reply should quote the rule, not the preference.
  • One call, with the trade-off in two columns: their approach and mine, cost of each.
  • If they will not talk: the lead mediates. I do not merge over the review, and I do not leave the PR blocked with no owner.
  • Whoever wins, the guideline gets one line so the next PR does not reopen it.

S5. How do you use AI tools, and where do you not trust them?

Say this: I use them for reading unfamiliar code, drafting tests, and boilerplate. I read every line before it is committed. I do not paste customer or account data into a tool the project has not approved.

  • Name the tools you actually use, and one real task for each (explore a module, generate the first test, sketch a mapper).
  • What I re-check myself: concurrency, money or balance math, security, and anything the model cannot run.
  • On this project the allowed tool is GitHub Copilot, with a monthly limit. Other tools are a question for the team, not a default.

Optional. AI tooling in practice

They may ask this after S5. It is not one of the scored S questions.

Which tools, and have you written rules or skills? Name the tool and the task. A rule or a skill should have one job: code style, architecture constraints, or how tests are generated. "I have a long prompt" is weaker than "I have a skill that loads only when I touch the network layer".

Have you connected AI to Jira, GitHub, or CI? Describe one integration end to end: what started it, what it was allowed to read, and what went wrong (permissions, a bad write, a token limit). Do not describe an integration you have not done.

Optional. What is in the model's context, and how do you keep it small?

Say this: Context is everything the model can see on that turn: the system rules, instruction files, skills that were loaded, the chat, tool calls and their results, and files that were read. All of it counts toward the window.

  • A skill is instructions loaded into the current chat when needed. A sub-agent is a separate chat that returns a summary. The main chat stays smaller. The cost is extra tokens, and the sub-agent does not see the rest of the conversation unless you pass it.
  • Usage is visible in the API response (input, output, cached) or in the tool's usage command. Cached tokens are the repeated prefix, not free work.
  • How I shrink it: short rule files, skills that load on demand, narrow file reads, a new session when the chat is long, and a sub-agent for a wide search.
